Why Bank Statements Come as PDFs (and Why That's Annoying)

Banks issue statements as PDFs because a PDF is a fixed, tamper-evident document — perfect for visa applications, loan files, and tax records. But the moment you want to actually use the numbers, a PDF fights you. Copy-pasting from a PDF mangles the columns into a mess.

Retyping months of transactions is nobody's idea of a good evening. Accountants charge by the hour for exactly this drudgery. This tool bridges the gap: it reads the text layer inside your statement PDF and rebuilds the transaction table as a spreadsheet-ready CSV you can download in seconds.

How It Works Under the Hood

Your PDF is parsed right in your browser with pdf.js, the open-source library Mozilla built for Firefox's own PDF viewer. The tool reads each page's text layer — every word plus its exact position on the page — then groups words into rows by vertical position and sorts each row left to right.

Rows that start with a date and contain an amount become transactions. Headers, footers, page numbers, and legal disclaimers are skipped automatically. Amount columns are found by clustering their horizontal positions across all pages, which is how debits, credits, and running balances land in the right columns even though every bank lays its statements out differently.

Step by Step: From Statement to Spreadsheet

First, download your statement from your bank as a PDF — the "download statement" or "export PDF" option in online banking, not a photo or scan. Drop it into the box above. The parsing library loads lazily at that moment only, so the page itself stays fast for everyone else.

Review the preview table next. Descriptions that wrapped across two lines are joined automatically, and every cell is editable — click any cell to fix a description or amount, or delete a row entirely. Then hit Download CSV. It opens in Excel, Google Sheets, or Numbers with a double-click, and totals, filters, and pivot tables all work on it like any spreadsheet.

Privacy: Nothing Uploads — and Why That Matters

A bank statement is a financial X-ray: every purchase, every paycheck, every transfer, all in one document. Most "PDF to Excel" sites make you upload that document to their server — and then put the result behind a signup or a paywall. Your most sensitive financial file ends up on a stranger's computer.

This page never sends your file anywhere. The parsing happens in JavaScript on your own device, and the only network request is fetching the pdf.js library itself from a CDN the first time you drop a file. You could unplug your internet after the page loads and the conversion would still work.

Common Issues, Honestly Explained

Two limitations, stated plainly. First, this works on text-based PDFs — what banks generate when you click "download statement." If your PDF is a scanned image (a photo of paper pages), there is no text layer to read. The tool will tell you so instead of failing silently; just re-download from your bank as a real PDF.

Second, statement layouts vary wildly between banks, so while the heuristics handle the common cases, always glance at the Debit and Credit columns before downloading. The editable preview exists exactly for that quick review. Password-protected PDFs can't be opened in the browser and get a clear error message telling you to remove the password first.

Why CSV and Not a Real Excel File

A CSV opens directly in Excel with a double-click, and it also works in Google Sheets, Numbers, and every accounting tool that imports spreadsheets. Generating a true .xlsx in the browser would mean shipping an 800KB library and its licensing baggage for almost no practical gain. If you specifically need the .xlsx format, open the CSV in Excel once and use Save As — two clicks.
