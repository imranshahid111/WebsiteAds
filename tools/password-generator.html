Why Password Length Beats Password Complexity

Ask most people what makes a strong password and they picture something like Tr0ub4dor&3 — short, hard to remember, hard to type, and surprisingly weak. Security researchers have measured this for years: every extra character multiplies the number of guesses an attacker needs, while clever substitutions like 0-for-O add almost nothing because attackers already try them. A 16-character password drawn from lowercase letters alone (26^16 possibilities) is enormously stronger than an 8-character password using every character type on the keyboard. Length is the lever that matters most — which is why this generator defaults to 16 and goes up to 64.

What "bits of entropy" means

The strength meter above measures entropy in bits. A password with 60 bits of entropy needs roughly 2^60 guesses to crack on average — about one quintillion. At 80 bits, cracking becomes practically impossible with any computing power you can buy today. The meter turns green around 60 bits and blue past 80. The rule of thumb: 60+ bits for everyday accounts, 80+ bits for email, banking, and anything that holds your money or identity.

Notice the pattern: the strongest passwords go on the accounts that unlock other accounts. Your email password is the single most important password you own, because "forgot password" links land there.

The three habits that actually prevent hacks

One: never reuse passwords. Data breaches happen constantly; attackers feed leaked username-password pairs into automated tools that try them on every major site. A unique password per site makes a breach at one service harmless to the rest. Two: use a password manager. No human can remember fifty unique 20-character passwords — managers generate, store, and autofill them, and you remember one master password. Three: turn on two-factor authentication for email, banking, and social accounts. Even if a password leaks, the second factor stops most attacks cold.

Common myths, corrected

Myth: changing passwords every 90 days keeps you safe. Forced rotation pushes people toward weak, predictable patterns (Password1 → Password2). Change a password when there is a breach, not on a calendar. Myth: security questions are a good backup. Your mother's maiden name is findable; use random answers stored in your password manager instead. Myth: biometrics replace passwords. Fingerprints are convenient but cannot be changed if compromised — they are a second factor, not a standalone secret.

How this generator works — and why you can trust it

Passwords here are generated with your browser's cryptographically secure random number generator (the Web Crypto API), the same source of randomness used by banks and security software — not the weaker Math.random() that some sites still use. Generation happens entirely on your device: the password is never transmitted, logged, or stored. You can verify this by disconnecting from the internet after the page loads — the generator keeps working. Generate a fresh password for each account, save it in a password manager, and never share passwords over email or chat.
