Best Free Pomodoro Apps & Timers for Studying in 2026

You sit down to study, open your laptop, and somehow an hour disappears into messages, videos, and tabs. The Pomodoro Technique fixes that with one simple rule: work for 25 focused minutes, take a 5-minute break, and repeat. After four rounds, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

The method was invented in the 1980s by Francesco Cirillo, who used a tomato-shaped kitchen timer — pomodoro is Italian for tomato. It is still one of the simplest study methods that actually works, because it turns a vague "study tonight" into a small, winnable sprint. The timer does the discipline for you: when it rings, you stop.

The problem is picking a timer. App stores are full of Pomodoro apps that call themselves free, then hide the basic cycle behind a paywall. This guide compares six genuinely free options, tested on what their free tier actually does in 2026.

How we compared them

Every app here runs a complete 25-minute focus session and a 5-minute break without asking for a card number — a timer that hides the basic cycle behind a paywall did not make the list. Then we scored each one on five things students actually care about.

The five checks: what works free and what is locked; how fast you get from opening the app to a running timer; platform; study extras like tasks or blocking; and the catch.

1. Pomofocus — best in a browser

Pomofocus opens straight into a 25-minute session with no signup screen in the way. You can add tasks, estimate how many pomodoros each one will take, and check them off as you finish — which turns the timer into a light planning tool. It also builds daily, weekly, and monthly focus reports from your sessions.

The catch: syncing across devices needs an account, and deeper report history is premium. For one browser on one machine, the free version is essentially the whole product. Desktop apps exist for Mac, Windows, and Linux.

2. Forest — best if your phone is the problem

Forest plants a virtual tree when you start a focus session. Leave the app to scroll social media and the tree dies. That small stake changes behaviour in a way a plain countdown never does, and over days and weeks you grow a forest that shows your discipline.

The free tier includes the core timer, Deep Focus app-blocking, and group focus sessions, plus Chrome and Firefox extensions. Earned coins fund real tree planting. The catch: tree species and some controls are paid, and there is no full desktop app — but if distraction is your main enemy, this is the timer to beat.

3. Focus Keeper — best free iPhone option

Focus Keeper is a free download for iPhone, iPad, and Apple Watch, with the standard 25-minute sessions, 5-minute short breaks, and a 20 to 30 minute long break after four rounds. The design is genuinely pleasant, and the free tier covers the full cycle with basic charts of your focus time.

One change deserves saying plainly: Pro used to be a small one-time purchase and is now a subscription from $3.99 a month, with a lifetime option up to $199.99. Treat the free tier as the product and it stays one of the best Pomodoro apps on iOS. Android users should look at Focus To-Do instead.

4. Marinara Timer — best for study groups, fully free

Marinara Timer is a long-running free tool with no payment tier at all — no signup, no premium, no upgrade ads. You pick a timer mode — Pomodoro, custom, or a shared kitchen timer — and it just runs.

Its strength is shared sessions: a study group can run the same timer in different places and take breaks together. The trade-off is that there are no tasks, no statistics, and no mobile app. If you want a timer and nothing else, that is a feature, not a flaw.

5. Focus To-Do — Pomodoro plus a task list

Focus To-Do combines a Pomodoro timer with a to-do list, so each 25-minute block is attached to a real task instead of floating in the abstract. That matters more than it sounds: "finish problem set 4" is a much better session goal than "study chemistry". The app runs on iOS, Android, and desktop.

The free version covers the core timer and tasks, with some advanced reports and themes behind a subscription. It is the best pick if you keep losing track of what each session was for — the task list turns a pile of pomodoros into visible progress.

Which one should you pick?

Match the app to your actual problem, not to the app store ratings. If your phone keeps pulling you away, pick Forest — the dying tree is the only feature here that punishes quitting. If you want the fastest possible start, pick Pomofocus or the free timer on this site: open the page, press start, done.

If you study on an iPhone and want a clean native app, pick Focus Keeper. If you study in a group, pick Marinara Timer and share the session. If every session needs a task attached, pick Focus To-Do.

One more honest note: the best timer is the one you open tomorrow. Students lose more study time choosing an app than any app ever saves. Pick one from the table above, use it for a week, and only switch if a specific feature is genuinely missing.

How to actually use a Pomodoro timer for studying

A timer only helps if the 25 minutes are real. Start each session by writing down one task — one chapter, one problem set, one essay section — and put your phone in another room or in Forest's Deep Focus mode. A vague goal like "study biology" is how a pomodoro turns into 25 minutes of reorganising notes.

Take the breaks seriously, and take them away from the screen. Stand up, get water, look out a window. A break spent scrolling is not a break: it drains the same attention the next session needs. After four rounds, take the full 15 to 30 minute long break, and eat something if you are hungry.

Track your count, not your hours. Four to eight focused sessions a day — roughly 2 to 4 hours of real study — beats ten distracted hours, and the number is easy to compare day to day.

Start with four sessions, hold that for a week, then add one at a time. If 25 minutes feels too short once the habit sticks, try 50-minute focus sessions with 10-minute breaks — the technique allows it, and many students prefer it for deep work.

Three mistakes undo most of the benefit. Skipping breaks to "push through" burns you out by the third session. Checking your phone during a break restarts the distraction loop. And running a timer while half-watching a video counts as neither studying nor resting. The rule is simple: the timer is on, the phone is away, one task at a time.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best free Pomodoro app for studying?

For most students, Pomofocus is the best free starting point: it runs in any browser with no signup, adds a task list, and keeps simple focus reports. If phone distraction is your main problem, Forest is better because leaving the app kills your tree. iPhone users should try Focus Keeper, and anyone who wants zero setup can use the free Pomodoro timer on this site.

Is the Pomodoro Technique 25 minutes of work and 5 minutes of rest?

Yes, that is the classic pattern: 25 minutes of focused work, then a 5-minute break, repeated four times, followed by a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. It was created by Francesco Cirillo in the 1980s using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. The lengths are adjustable — many students do better with 50-minute focus sessions once the habit sticks.

Are free Pomodoro timers really free, or is there a catch?

The timers in this guide all run a full 25/5 cycle without paying. The usual catch is that extras sit behind a subscription: deeper statistics, syncing across devices, themes, or app blocking. Browser timers like Pomofocus and Marinara Timer stay closest to fully free, while mobile apps have drifted toward subscriptions.

Can I use a Pomodoro timer without installing an app?

Yes. Browser timers need no install and no account: Pomofocus and Marinara Timer open straight into a countdown, and the free Pomodoro Study Timer on this site works the same way, with custom durations, a session plan, and an end-of-phase alarm. A browser timer is also the best choice on a shared or college computer.

How many Pomodoros should a student do per day?

Four to eight focused sessions — roughly 2 to 4 hours of real study — is a realistic daily target for most students, and it beats ten distracted hours. Start with four, track the count for a week, and add one session at a time. Quality matters more than the number: a Pomodoro only counts if the phone stayed away for the full 25 minutes.
