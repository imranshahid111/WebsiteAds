ChatGPT vs Claude vs Gemini for Students: An Honest Comparison

Every student group chat has the same argument: which chatbot is actually best for school? ChatGPT has the biggest name, Claude has the most loyal fans, and Gemini keeps getting better inside Google's apps. All three are free, and all three reshuffle their free plans every few months.

There is no single winner — but there is a right answer for each school task. This comparison uses the current free tiers (checked late October 2026), says plainly where the providers publish their limits and where the numbers are estimates, and ends with which tool to reach for first. Treat exact figures as a snapshot and verify them inside your own account.

The short answer

In a hurry? Here it is. Pick ChatGPT when you want an always-available study partner with no daily message counter. Pick Claude when your work is a stack of long readings or PDFs. Pick Gemini when you live in Google Docs and need free deep research or image generation.

That is the honest summary. The sections below explain why — and where each one falls short, because the weaknesses decide more than the strengths.

ChatGPT: the always-on study partner

ChatGPT's free tier changed dramatically in August 2026, when OpenAI removed the text-message counter entirely: everyday text chats are now unlimited on the free plan, subject to abuse-prevention safeguards. For a student, that is the single biggest free-tier upgrade any of the three made this year — you can ask your fiftieth follow-up question at midnight before an exam without counting.

The free model is GPT-6 Luna, a current model rather than a leftover. It handles the classic student jobs well: explaining a concept three different ways, turning notes into practice questions, checking your reasoning step by step. If you want one chatbot you never have to think about, this is it.

The catches are real. Free uploads are capped at 3 files a day, so it is a poor pick for working through a folder of lecture PDFs. Image generation, voice and deep research exist on free but are visibly rationed — the in-product meter, not a published number, tells you when you hit the wall. And the free plan shows ads, which neither rival does.

On the paid side, ChatGPT Go ($8/month) and Plus ($20/month) unlock stronger models and bigger tool allowances, and OpenAI runs seasonal student promotions — check the current offer page, since the terms and deadlines change by semester.

Claude: the careful reader

Claude's free plan is the pick for reading-heavy courses. It runs Claude Sonnet 5.5 (plus Haiku 5.5 for quick replies) and accepts up to 20 files per chat, with PDFs up to about 1,000 pages — the most generous document handling of the three free tiers. For a student facing a 200-page reading packet, that is a decisive difference.

Claude's other signature strength is tone: it writes and summarises in a calm, structured way that stays close to the source. Ask it for the argument, the evidence and what is disputed in a long article, and the summary reads like good study notes rather than a confident-sounding guess. That does not make it accurate — it still invents facts — but the structure makes its claims easier to check.

The price of that quality is a rolling 5-hour usage window instead of ChatGPT's unlimited text. Anthropic publishes no fixed message count; in practice, students report roughly 15–40 messages per window depending on message length and server load. Long conversations also burn the allowance faster, because each new message reprocesses the whole thread — start a fresh chat per topic and your free quota lasts much longer.

Two more honest limits: Claude has no image generation at all, and its Research feature is paid-only. If your assignment needs diagrams or deep web research, pair it with one of the other two. On privacy, Claude lets you opt your chats out of training with one setting — worth doing.

The paid ladder is the simplest of the three: Claude Pro ($20/month, or $17 billed annually) gives roughly five times the usage plus the stronger Opus model. There is no mid-tier and no student plan.

Gemini: the Google-native researcher

Gemini's free tier is the sleeper pick, especially if your school life runs on Google Docs, Drive and Gmail. It works naturally alongside those apps, summarises YouTube videos (handy for lecture recordings), and — unlike the other two free tiers — includes both Deep Research and image generation with editing.

Deep Research on free is the standout. You describe a topic, it browses and reads sources, then returns a cited report. No fixed free count is published, and it can be unavailable at peak times, but for building the source list of a research paper it beats asking a plain chatbot for "references" — the answers come from pages it actually opened.

File handling is generous: up to 10 files per prompt. The free context window is 32K tokens — smaller than Claude's paid ceiling, but plenty for most assignments. The catch is the limit style: a compute-based allowance that refreshes every five hours, with a weekly cap underneath, and Google does not publish the exact numbers. In practice it feels fine for daily study use and tight during deadline crunches.

One student deal is worth knowing: Google offers Google AI Pro free for a year to US college students 18 and older (redeem by December 31, 2026; a card is required and it renews at $19.99/month unless cancelled). That is the most generous student offer of the three this school year — set a cancellation reminder if you claim it.

Gemini also has the cheapest paid ladder: Google AI Plus ($4.99/month) doubles free usage, and AI Pro ($19.99/month) quadruples it with a 1M-token window.

The honest way to use them

The rules from our student tools guide apply double here. Use the chatbots to learn, practise and check your own work — and ask your teacher before using them to produce work you submit. The one-line test still works: if you could not explain a sentence out loud without the tool, you have not learned it yet.

The specific trap in a comparison like this is treating the tools as sources. None of them is. Every model on this page invents citations, dates and quotes that look perfect — Gemini's Deep Research is the partial exception, because it shows the pages it read, and you should still open them. Never copy a reference from a chatbot into an assignment without checking the real source.

If a class allows AI use with disclosure, say exactly what you used it for: "used ChatGPT to brainstorm the outline; wrote all prose myself." Vague disclosure ("AI was used") helps nobody. Keep your prompts and the tool's answers until the grade is final — some schools ask for them.

A rotation that costs $0

Here is the setup that gets the most from all three free tiers. Use ChatGPT as the default: unlimited text means it never interrupts a study session. When you hit its 3-file daily upload cap or need a long reading digested, switch to Claude. When you need sources researched or an image made, switch to Gemini.

This rotation also dodges the biggest free-tier annoyance: the 5-hour windows on Claude and Gemini. One tool's cooldown is another tool's uptime. And because all three are free without a credit card, there is no billing to manage — just three bookmarks.

Track your grades, not your tools. Frequently asked questions

Which is best for students: ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini?

For most students: ChatGPT as the everyday default (unlimited free text chat), Claude for long readings and PDFs, and Gemini for research and image generation. Used by task, two of the three beat any single pick.

Is ChatGPT really unlimited on the free plan?

Everyday text chats are unlimited since August 2026, subject to abuse-prevention safeguards. File uploads (3 per day), image generation, voice and deep research have separate, smaller limits.

Can Claude handle a whole textbook chapter?

Its free tier accepts up to 20 files per chat with PDFs up to about 1,000 pages — enough for a chapter or a full reading packet. Keep one chat per reading so the rolling 5-hour usage window lasts.

Does Gemini's free plan include Deep Research?

Yes — unlike ChatGPT's limited version and Claude's paid-only Research. No fixed free count is published and it can be unavailable at peak times, but it reads real pages and returns a cited report.

Will my teacher know I used AI?

Possibly. AI detectors are unreliable, but teachers notice style changes and invented citations. The safe path is your school's policy: learn with the tools, disclose when required, and never submit text you could not explain yourself.
