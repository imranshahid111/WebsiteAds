How to Write AI Video Prompts That Don't Look Fake

Scroll any feed for five minutes and you can spot AI video instantly. The faces are too smooth, the hands melt between frames, the light feels wrong somehow. Most creators blame the tool — and most of the time, the tool is innocent. The difference between "obviously AI" and "wait, was that real?" is usually five or six missing details in the prompt.

This guide comes from real testing: prompts tried on current generators, failures kept, and the patterns that repeat every time. You will learn the five giveaways that mark a video as fake, the exact anatomy of a prompt that looks real, the camera language tools actually understand, and a copyable template to start from. The 5 giveaways that mark a video as fake

Every "fake-looking" video fails in one of five ways. Learn to name the failure, and the prompt already contains half the fix.

Morphing faces and handsThe model invents detail frame by frame, and guesses differently each timeFewer close-ups; profiles, over-shoulder angles, "face in soft focus"

If your video looks fake, it is almost always one of these five. Fix them in the prompt before you blame the generator.

The anatomy of a prompt that looks real

Every realistic prompt has five parts, in this order. Subject: who or what, with physical detail. Action: one clear motion. Camera: lens, angle, and a single move. Light: source, direction, and quality. Environment: where, with texture. Finish with a negative line — what the model must not do.

Here is a bad prompt: "A man carving wood in a workshop." It gives the model nothing, so the model fills every gap with defaults: perfect light, a spotless bench, flawless hands, a face from a catalogue. That is where the fake look comes from — not the model, but the empty space you left it.

Now the same idea, written properly: "Slow push-in macro close-up of weathered hands carving a small wooden car from a walnut block with a chisel, wood shavings curling off the blade, cluttered garage workshop, morning window light from the left, dust floating in the air, natural skin texture, hands stay on the wood, no text, no watermark."

Every clause closes a gap the model would otherwise guess: the wood, the tool, the byproduct, the light source and direction, an imperfection, the camera move, and two hard negatives. That gap-closing is the whole trick — realism is what remains when the model has nothing left to invent.

Camera language the tools actually understand

Generators understand simple camera moves far better than adjectives. "Slow push-in", "orbit around the subject", "overhead crane descending", "macro close-up with rack focus" — concrete moves, one per shot. Two moves in one prompt ("push in, then orbit left") confuses the model and is a top cause of morphing.

Static shots are underrated. A locked-off camera with motion inside the frame — steam rising, shavings falling, curtains moving — reads as far more real than any camera move, because the model invents fewer frames. When a prompt fails, retry the same scene with "static camera" before changing anything else.

Write physics into the prompt

Video models do not know gravity — you have to tell them. Words that anchor reality: heavy, grounded, feet planted, hands gripping, weight shifting. "A man jumps" is a coin flip; "a man jumps, dust puffing where his boots leave the ground, landing in a crouch that absorbs the impact" tells the model what happens at both ends of the motion.

Keep one action per shot. Every extra action ("she opens the door, walks out, and waves") multiplies the morphing risk. Professionals chain short, consistent shots instead: paste the same subject description word-for-word in each prompt and change only the action and the camera.

The text-and-faces rule

Three rules, all from painful experience. Never ask for on-screen text — video models still render gibberish lettering; add titles in your editor later. Avoid extreme close-ups of invented faces — prefer profiles, backs, over-the-shoulder angles, or keep faces small in the frame. And if the face matters, keep the camera still: movement plus a face is where morphing lives.

Continuity: making shot 2 match shot 1

The fake look often arrives between shots, not inside one. Paste the same subject description verbatim in every prompt of a sequence — "weathered hands, walnut block, blue garage apron" — because the model has no memory; your text is its memory. Keep the same light direction, room, and clothes; change only the action and the camera.

Some tools offer seed locking or reference images — use them when available. But the portable version of consistency is text discipline: identical nouns across prompts. Test it yourself: generate three shots with a pasted subject line and three without, and the difference is obvious.

The copyable prompt recipe

  • Camera: one move, e.g. "slow push-in macro close-up"
  • Subject: with two physical details, e.g. "weathered hands, rolled sleeves"
  • Action: one action with a byproduct, e.g. "carving a wooden car, shavings curling off the blade"
  • Place: with two texture details, e.g. "cluttered garage workshop, oil-stained bench"
  • Light: source plus direction, e.g. "morning window light from the left, soft shadows"
  • Imperfection: one flaw, e.g. "dust floating in the air"
  • Negatives: "no text, no watermark, hands stay consistent"

When it still looks fake: the 5-minute checklist

Run failed clips through this list before regenerating — nine times out of ten, one line of the prompt is the culprit.

  • Skin looks plastic → add texture words: pores, grain, sweat, wrinkles.
  • Faces or hands morph → static camera, fewer close-ups, profile angles.
  • Motion feels floaty → weight and contact words: grounded, gripping, planted.
  • Light feels flat → name the source, direction, and shadow quality.
  • Everything too clean → imperfections: worn, dusty, cluttered, asymmetric.
  • Gibberish text appears → delete all text requests from the prompt.
  • Shots don't match → paste the identical subject line in every prompt.

Frequently asked questions

Why do my AI videos always look fake?

Because the prompt leaves gaps the model fills with stock-photo defaults. Add concrete subject detail, a named light source and direction, one camera move, physics words like weight and contact, and hard negatives such as "no text, no watermark".

Can a good prompt really fix morphing faces and hands?

Mostly. Fewer face close-ups, a static camera, and profile or over-the-shoulder angles remove most morphing, because the model has fewer invented frames to guess. The remaining quality ceiling belongs to the model itself.

Should I write AI video prompts in English?

Yes. Video generators are trained overwhelmingly on English text, so English prompts produce more predictable results than other languages, even when you describe non-English subjects.

How long should an AI video prompt be?

About 40–80 words is the sweet spot: roughly one detailed clause each for subject, action, camera, light, environment, and negatives. Shorter prompts force the model to guess; longer ones dilute the important details.
