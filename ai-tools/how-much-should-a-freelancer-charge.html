How Much Should a Freelancer Charge in 2026?

Most freelancers pick a rate the same way: take the salary they want, divide by 2,080 working hours, and quote the result. It feels mathematical and fair. It is also the single most reliable way to undercharge, because almost none of the assumptions behind it are true for self-employed work.

This guide gives you the formula that actually works — income goal plus expenses, divided by the hours you can realistically bill — with a worked example, typical 2026 rate ranges by role, and a plain-English plan for raising your rates. Why salary ÷ 2,080 guarantees undercharging

The 2,080-hour number comes from 40 hours times 52 weeks. That is a full-time employee's year: paid holidays, paid sick days, an employer covering half the payroll tax, and health insurance partly subsidized. None of that applies to you. As a freelancer, every week off is unpaid, every invoice you do not send is income that does not exist.

Start with billable hours. A freelancer who works 40 hours a week typically bills 24 to 28 of them. The rest goes to finding clients, writing proposals, bookkeeping, learning, and answering emails nobody pays for. Multiply 26 billable hours by 48 working weeks (four weeks off, unpaid) and you get 1,248 hours — not 2,080. Every calculation built on 2,080 is off by roughly 40% before it starts.

Then come the costs an employer used to absorb. Self-employment tax in the US is 15.3% on the first $176,100 of net earnings, health insurance for an individual averages several hundred dollars a month, and there is no 401(k) match, no paid leave, no equipment budget. A $100,000 salary converted to $48 an hour is not the freelance equivalent of $100,000 — it is a pay cut wearing a nicer number.

The real formula for a freelance rate

Forget salary math. Build the rate from your own life, in three numbers. First, your annual income goal: the take-home pay you want, after taxes, comparable to the life you want to live. Second, your annual business expenses: software, hardware, insurance, accounting, coworking, and a buffer for slow months. Third, your billable hours: the hours you can genuinely invoice, not the hours you sit at a desk.

The formula is: (income goal + expenses + taxes set aside) ÷ billable hours. The taxes part trips people up, so plan for it explicitly. A simple approach is to add 25 to 30% to your income goal as the tax reserve, then divide. It is rough, but rough and included beats precise and forgotten.

The result is your floor rate — the minimum you can charge without quietly losing money. Your quote rate should sit 10 to 20% above it, because proposals get negotiated down and scope always creeps. Notice what this does to the naive answer. An $80,000 "salary" divided by 2,080 is $38 an hour. The real freelance equivalent, for 20 billable hours a week and four unpaid weeks off, is roughly triple that. That gap is the difference between a freelance business that survives and one that slowly bleeds out.

The biggest lever in the formula is billable hours, not the income goal. Going from 15 to 20 billable hours a week drops the required rate by a quarter. That is why capacity matters more than courage: before you raise prices, check whether you are actually filling the hours you count. Treat these ranges as market context, not targets. A rate that is "typical" for your role but below your floor rate from the formula is still a rate that loses you money. The formula always wins the argument: it is built from your costs, while market ranges are built from other people's.

Two things push you to the top of any range: specialization and proof. A copywriter who writes SaaS onboarding emails with case studies charges double a generalist with the same years of experience. If you are new, pick a narrow niche fast, collect proof aggressively, and let the formula protect you while the proof compounds.

Hourly, project, or retainer: picking your pricing model

Hourly pricing is the safest when scope is uncertain or the work is open-ended — maintenance, support, consulting. Its downside is that it punishes efficiency: the faster you get, the less you earn. Use hourly when the client cannot describe the deliverable clearly enough for a fixed price.

Project pricing wins when the deliverable is defined: a website, a logo, ten blog posts. You price the value and the risk, and every efficiency gain is yours to keep. The key discipline is a written scope with revision limits. Most project-pricing disasters are not pricing failures — they are scope failures, where "just one more change" was never priced.

Retainers are the best income a freelancer can have: a fixed monthly fee for guaranteed availability or a bundle of work. They smooth the feast-or-famine cycle and make revenue predictable. Offer them to clients who come back regularly, price them at a small discount to your hourly rate, and cap the included hours so unused time does not roll over forever.

Pricing mistakes that keep freelancers broke

The first mistake is anchoring on the first client. A desperate early project at $25 an hour becomes the reference price in your head for years. Reset it deliberately: recalculate the formula every time your goal or costs change, and treat the first client's price as what it was — a learning discount with an expiry date.

The second mistake is free scope creep. "Can you just also..." is the most expensive sentence in freelancing. Every extra round of revisions, every added feature, every rush delivery has a price. Name it before you do the work, in writing. Clients who respect your rate respect your scope; the rest were never going to be good clients.

The third mistake is never raising rates. Inflation, better skills, fuller calendars — all of them mean last year's rate is a discount this year. If your calendar has been full for two months straight, you are underpriced. The fix is a raise, not longer hours, because your hours are the one inventory you cannot restock.

The fourth mistake is discounting without trading. When a client asks for a lower price, the answer is a smaller scope, not the same work for less money. Removing a deliverable or two revision rounds keeps the rate intact and teaches the client that price follows scope — which makes every future negotiation easier.

How to raise your rates without losing clients

Announce the increase in writing, 30 days ahead, plainly and without apology. A single sentence does it: "Starting March 1, my rate moves from $X to $Y." Most long-term clients expect periodic increases the way they expect any supplier's prices to move. Over-explaining invites negotiation; a clean statement usually gets a clean acceptance.

For your best long-term clients, consider a grace period — the current project or the next quarter at the old rate. It costs you little and it signals that you value the relationship. But set the date in the same email. A grace period without an end date becomes the new permanent price.

Some clients will leave. That is not a pricing failure — it is market information. If a fair increase empties your pipeline, the real problem is client concentration, not your rate. The freelance businesses that charge well share one trait: more prospects than they can take, so losing any single client is a scheduling event, not a crisis.

And protect the number with a margin. Your rate has to survive taxes, dry months, and rate negotiation — which is why the profit margin calculator mindset applies to services too. If a project lands under your floor rate after all costs, it is not cheap marketing. It is a donation.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a beginner freelancer charge per hour?

Start from your income goal, not from what others charge. Add your annual expenses to your target take-home, then divide by the hours you can realistically bill. Most beginners land between $30 and $60 an hour — below that, taxes and unpaid time off usually mean you are working for less than minimum wage.

Why is salary divided by 2,080 the wrong way to set a freelance rate?

The 2,080-hour figure assumes you bill every working hour of a full-time job. Freelancers typically bill 60-70% of working hours, pay their own health insurance and self-employment tax (15.3%), and take unpaid time off. That math is why $100K salary converted to $48/hr as a freelancer is usually a pay cut, not an equivalent.

How do I raise my freelance rates with existing clients?

Give 30 days of notice, grandfather current clients for one project or quarter if needed, and state the new rate plainly without apologizing. Most long-term clients expect periodic increases. If a client leaves over a fair increase, that is a signal your pipeline needs more clients, not that your rate was wrong.

Should I charge hourly, per project, or a monthly retainer?

Charge hourly when the scope is uncertain or ongoing, per project when you can define the deliverable clearly (you keep the efficiency gains), and retainer when a client needs steady monthly access (you get predictable income). Most successful freelancers use all three, depending on the client.

How often should a freelancer review their rate?

Recalculate whenever your income goal changes, your expenses rise, or your billable hours drop. A formal review every six to twelve months is enough for most freelancers. If your calendar is full for two months straight, you are underpriced — raise the rate instead of adding more hours.
