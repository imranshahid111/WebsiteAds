Password Security for Beginners: How Long Should a Password Be?

Your password is the only thing standing between a stranger and your email, your bank account, and your photos. Most people still protect all of that with a pet's name and a birth year — and then reuse the same password on twenty sites. This guide explains, in plain English, how long a password should be, what actually makes one strong, and how to fix yours in about ten minutes.

The short answer: aim for at least 15 characters, make every password unique, and store them in a password manager. If you only remember one number from this article, make it 15. The rest of this guide shows why that number, why the old advice about symbols and 90-day changes is out of date, and what to do instead.

How long should a password be?

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) sets the standard most security teams follow. Its current guidance, SP 800-63B Revision 4, finalised in July 2025, says a password used on its own should be at least 15 characters long. If the password is backed by multi-factor authentication (MFA), the minimum drops to 8 characters.

Fifteen characters sounds long until you compare it with the alternative. A password of 8 random characters is crackable; a password of 15 random characters is not, in any practical sense. Length is the single biggest lever you have, bigger than adding symbols or capital letters. Every extra character multiplies the guessing work an attacker faces.

For everyday use, treat the numbers like this: 8 characters is the floor, never the goal. Twelve characters is good for low-risk accounts like a newsletter login. Sixteen or more is the standard for email, banking, and anything tied to money. And if you use a password manager, length costs you nothing — the manager remembers it, not you.

How long does it take to crack a password?

Hive Systems publishes a yearly table that measures how long it takes a powerful computer to guess every possible password. Its 2025 table assumes twelve top-end graphics cards (RTX 5090s) attacking a password stored with bcrypt, the slow hashing method most sites use. The results show why length wins.

PasswordTime to crack (2025, 12 × RTX 5090, bcrypt)
8 characters, lowercase + numbers3 weeks
8 characters, upper + lower + numbers62 years
8 characters, full mix (upper, lower, numbers, symbols)164 years
12 characters, full mixMillions of years
18 characters, numbers only284,000 years

Two things jump out. First, adding characters beats adding character types: 18 digits outlast 8 mixed characters by an enormous margin. Second, these times only apply to random passwords that have never leaked. If your password appears in a breach list or is a common word with a number on the end, an attacker does not brute-force it at all — they just try the list, and it falls in seconds.

The old rules are dead: what NIST changed in 2025

If your work or school still forces a capital letter, a symbol, and a new password every 90 days, its policy is out of date. NIST's 2025 revision bans mandatory complexity rules outright — systems "shall not" require a mix of character types — and bans forced periodic changes unless there is evidence of compromise.

The old rules backfired. Forced symbols produced passwords like "Summer2024!" — predictable to attackers and hard for people to remember. Forced rotation produced "Password1" becoming "Password2". NIST now requires the opposite: check every new password against a list of known-breached passwords, accept at least 64 characters, allow pasting, and let password managers work.

One rule did survive, and it matters more than the rest: use a different password for every account. Breaches are routine — billions of credentials circulate in leaked databases — and attackers automate "credential stuffing", trying a leaked password on every major site. A unique password turns a breach at one service into a non-event everywhere else.

Three rules that actually keep you safe

Rule one: never reuse a password. This is the single highest-impact habit in password security, and it is also the hardest without help — nobody can memorise fifty unique passwords. That is exactly what a password manager is for.

Rule two: use a password manager. It generates long random passwords, stores them encrypted, and fills them in for you. You remember one strong master password (or use your fingerprint), and the manager handles the rest. NIST's 2025 guidance effectively requires systems to support managers, including paste and long passwords.

Rule three: turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) for email, banking, and social accounts. Even if a password leaks, a second factor — an authenticator app or a security key — stops most attacks. Start with email: it is the key to resetting every other account you own.

How to build a strong password you can remember

For the few passwords you must type yourself — your password manager's master password, your phone PIN, your laptop login — use a passphrase: four or five random words strung together. "Correct horse battery staple" is famous for a reason, but pick your own words: "purple-tractor-mango-river-42" is long, memorable, and hard to guess.

Avoid anything personal: names, birthdays, teams, and pet names are the first things an attacker tries, and most of them are visible on your social media. Avoid keyboard patterns ("qwerty123") and common substitutions ("P@ssw0rd!") — attackers know every trick in the book, because the book is public.

When should you change a password?

Change a password when there is a real reason: the service tells you about a breach, a breach-checker flags your address, you spot a login you did not make, or you shared the password with someone. Outside those cases, a long unique password does not need a calendar-driven change.

Check whether your email has appeared in a breach with a free service like Have I Been Pwned. If it has, change that password and any other account that shared it — then turn on 2FA. Treat a breach alert as a to-do list, not a panic: work through the flagged accounts one by one.

Passkeys: the password's replacement

The biggest change in 2025 and 2026 is passkeys. A passkey replaces your password with a cryptographic key stored on your phone or computer; you sign in with your fingerprint or face, and there is nothing to type, phish, or leak. Google, Apple, and Microsoft all support them now.

Where a site offers a passkey, take it — it is stronger than any password. But passkeys are not everywhere yet, and passwords will share the job for years. The right setup in 2026 is passkeys where offered, long unique passwords everywhere else, and 2FA on the accounts that matter most.

Your 10-minute password fix

Start with email, because it resets everything else: give it a unique 16+ character password and turn on 2FA. Then do the same for your bank and your main social accounts. Install a password manager and move your logins into it as you use them — there is no need to fix all fifty in one sitting.

Finally, run a breach check on your main email address and change anything flagged. Ten minutes of work today removes the most common way accounts actually get stolen: a reused password from an old breach, tried on a site that matters. Frequently asked questions

How long should a password be in 2026?

At least 15 characters when the password is the only login factor, according to NIST SP 800-63B Revision 4 (July 2025). Eight characters is the minimum when the password is backed by multi-factor authentication. For email and banking, 16 or more characters is a sensible standard.

Is a 12-character password strong enough?

A random 12-character password with mixed character types takes millions of years to brute-force with current hardware, so it is strong against guessing. The bigger risk is reuse or a breach: a 12-character password that you reuse everywhere is weaker in practice than a unique 16-character one.

Do I still need special characters in my password?

No — NIST's 2025 guidance says systems shall not require symbols, capitals, or numbers. Special characters are allowed and help, but length matters more. A long passphrase of plain words beats a short password full of symbols.

How often should I change my password?

Only when there is evidence of compromise: a breach alert, a suspicious login, or a service telling you to. Forced changes every 60 or 90 days are no longer recommended, because they push people toward weak, predictable passwords.

Are password managers safe?

Yes. A reputable password manager stores your passwords encrypted, and you unlock it with one strong master password. It is far safer than reusing a few memorable passwords — and NIST's current guidance expects systems to support managers, including paste and long passwords.
