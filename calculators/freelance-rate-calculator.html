Why Your Hourly Rate Is Not Your Salary ÷ 2,080

The most expensive math mistake a new freelancer makes is taking their old salary, dividing by 2,080 working hours, and calling it a rate. A $75,000 salary becomes "$36 an hour" — and then the freelancer slowly goes broke. The 2,080 number assumes every work hour is a paid hour, which is true for employees and false for everyone else.

Three things a salary quietly covers that your rate must now cover openly: unpaid time (vacation, holidays, sick days, plus the pitching, proposals, admin and invoicing that clients never see), the employer half of your taxes, and your overhead (software subscriptions, hardware, insurance, coworking, your own training). Skip any one of them and the rate looks fine on paper while your bank account shrinks.

The inputs, explained

Target annual income: what you want to keep for yourself after business costs — think of it as the salary you are replacing, before taxes. Weeks off: be honest here. Four weeks of vacation plus a week of holidays plus a sick week is six unpaid weeks, and most freelancers underestimate this.

Billable hours per week: hours a client actually pays for — not your 40-hour work week. If you work 40 hours but 15 go to marketing, admin and proposals, your billable number is 25. Business expenses: everything the work costs you per year — Adobe or Figma subscriptions, a laptop replacement fund, liability insurance, coworking fees.

Tax allowance: the slice of each invoice you must not spend. In the US, self-employment tax alone is 15.3% of net earnings, and income tax sits on top — many freelancers set aside 25–30% of every payment. The default 15% here is a conservative starting estimate; adjust it once you know your real tax picture.

The math: how the floor rate works

The formula is: (target income + annual expenses) × (1 + tax allowance) ÷ billable hours per year. First we add your income goal to your costs — that is the real amount the business must earn. Then we gross it up for taxes, because a $75,000 take-home goal at a 15% allowance means the business must collect about $92,000. Finally we divide by your actual billable hours (billable weeks × billable hours per week) — not 2,080.

The recommended rate is the floor plus a 15% buffer. That buffer is not greed: it is negotiation room, scope-creep insurance, and a cushion for slow months. Quote with the recommended rate. The floor is private — it is simply the line below which you are subsidizing the client.

A worked example

Take a designer who wants $75,000 a year, takes 4 weeks off, bills 25 hours a week, spends $5,000 a year on tools and insurance, and sets aside 15% for taxes. Her billable year is 48 weeks × 25 hours = 1,200 hours. The business must earn ($75,000 + $5,000) × 1.15 = $92,000. Her floor rate is $92,000 ÷ 1,200 = $76.67/hour, and her recommended quote rate is about $88/hour.

Now compare that to the naive salary math: $75,000 ÷ 2,080 = $36.06. The real floor is more than double the naive number — which is exactly why so many talented freelancers feel busy all year and still can't save.

Three reasons freelancers undercharge

1. They price the work week, not the billable week. "I work 40 hours" becomes 40 billable hours in the math, but half of those hours are invisible labor. Count only hours clients pay for. 2. They forget the employer's half of everything. Your old employer paid half your Social Security and Medicare, gave you paid leave, and bought your laptop. Those costs did not disappear — they moved to you.

3. They anchor to the cheapest competitor. Someone on a marketplace charges $20/hour because they live somewhere cheaper, are learning, or are also going broke. Price your own numbers, not theirs.

How to use the two numbers in a quote

When a client asks your rate, start from the recommended rate and quote the project, not the hours, whenever you can: "This project is $2,200" lands better than "$88 an hour." If they push for hourly, quote the recommended rate as your standard and let negotiation pull toward — but never below — the floor. If a project forces you below the floor, the honest answer is no; taking it means working at a loss and resenting the client.

Track your actual billable hours for a month and rerun the calculator with real numbers — most people's first estimate is optimistic, and the rate usually goes up, not down.

Next steps after your calculation

Run your numbers, then write the recommended rate on a sticky note and quote it for the next 30 days without flinching — the first quote at a real rate is the hardest. Put the floor rate somewhere private as your walk-away line.

Then make the income real: run our 50/30/20 budget calculator with your new freelance income to split it into needs, wants and savings, and feed the savings slice into our savings goal calculator to see how fast an emergency fund builds from freelance earnings. Revisit this calculator every 6 months or after any big life change — a new expense, a tax surprise, or a week of unpaid illness all change the floor.
