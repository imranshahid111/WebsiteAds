Gross Margin vs Net Margin: The Two Numbers That Decide Whether Your Business Survives

Most small business owners know their revenue. Far fewer know their margin — and margin, not revenue, decides who stays open. A bakery doing $20,000 a month and keeping $800 is one bad month away from closing; a freelancer billing $6,000 and keeping $4,500 is quietly rich. The difference is margin: the percentage of every dollar that is profit, not just passing through. Gross margin = (revenue − product cost) ÷ revenue. It answers: "Does my pricing work before overhead?" Net margin = (gross profit − all overhead) ÷ revenue. It answers: "What does the business actually keep?" You need both. A healthy gross margin with a terrible net margin means your pricing is fine but your overhead is eating the business — which is a completely different problem from pricing too low.

The inputs, explained

Selling price per unit: what the customer pays, before discounts. If you run frequent sales, use your average realized price, not the sticker price. Cost per unit: everything the item itself costs you — materials, manufacturing, wholesale price, packaging. Not rent, not your salary — just the variable cost of one more unit. Units sold per month: your average monthly volume; seasonal businesses should use a typical month, not the holiday peak. Monthly overhead: every fixed cost of keeping the doors open — rent, wages, software subscriptions, shipping accounts, payment processing fees, insurance. Be thorough: the number most owners underestimate is fees, which typically skim 2.9% + 30¢ per transaction and vanish into the background.

A worked example

Take a small skincare brand selling a moisturizer at $49.99, costing $22 to make, selling 500 units a month, with $3,000 in monthly overhead. Revenue is 500 × $49.99 = $24,995. Product cost is 500 × $22 = $11,000. Gross profit = $13,995, a 56.0% gross margin — pricing works. Subtract the $3,000 overhead and net profit is $10,995, a 44.0% net margin. That is a strong business. Now drop the price to $44.99 to "be competitive": net margin falls to about 37.8% — roughly $1,600 less kept every month — without a single cost changing. Price moves hit margin harder than almost anything else, which is why the scenario table above exists.

Three ways to improve a weak margin

1. Raise the price (try it in the table first). A 5–10% increase rarely loses 5–10% of customers, and it lifts every dollar of revenue. Add value instead of apologizing for it — bundle, improve packaging, add a guarantee. 2. Cut the real unit cost. Renegotiate with suppliers, order in larger batches, simplify packaging. But know the floor: cheaper materials can cheapen the product and cost you customers. 3. Trim overhead, not muscle. Cancel unused subscriptions, renegotiate processing fees and rent, automate one repetitive task. Do not cut the marketing that brings the sales — a 44% margin on zero revenue is zero dollars.

The margin trap: looking profitable while cash is empty

A positive margin does not mean money is in the bank. Margin is a ratio — cash is a timing problem. Inventory ties up cash for months, customers pay on 30-day terms while your suppliers want 15, and debt payments hit whether sales arrive or not. If your margin looks fine but the account is dry, the fix is cash flow, not pricing: invoice immediately, shorten payment terms, trim inventory, and separate business money from personal money so the profit is visible.
